The gym Meet the owner.

Josh runs CrossFit Prior Lake, a CrossFit affiliate in a commuter town south-west of the Twin Cities. It is the kind of gym the model was built for: a coached class, a whiteboard, a room where people know each other's names and notice when someone stops turning up. Most affiliate owners are also the billing department, the programmer, the person who places the retail order and the one who runs the Instagram. That is roughly the job description across North America.

He was sitting at 81 members. For an affiliate that is a real business and a real community, but it is also the number where the maths gets tight: every member who moves away, gets injured or quietly stops coming is a visible dent in the roster, and there is nothing spare to absorb it.

The starting point The problem.

A gym sitting on a number like that rarely has a coaching problem. It has a top-of-funnel problem and a follow-up problem, in that order: not enough strangers hearing about the place, and too slow a reply to the ones who do.

Word of mouth is the best lead source there is, and it is also the one you cannot turn up. It arrives when it arrives. A gym running mostly on referrals grows in steps and then sits on a plateau, busy enough to feel fine, flat enough that the membership number barely moves from one quarter to the next.

Members81Plateau · word of mouth only

Eighty-one is a comfortable number to sit still at.

Underneath that sits the second, quieter problem, and it is the one we find at nearly every gym we talk to. The enquiries that do arrive, a form fill at 9pm or a DM on a Saturday, get answered when the owner comes off the floor. Sometimes that is an hour. Sometimes it is the next day. By then the person has usually messaged the other gyms nearby, and whoever replied first has already booked them in.

What we ran What we did.

Three pieces, built in one pass and switched on together. None of them is clever. The value is that all three exist at once, which is rare.

Intro bookedthree pieces, one launch

  1. Local paid traffic

    Meta ads on Facebook and Instagram, aimed at a radius people will actually drive for. Nobody joins a CrossFit gym forty minutes from their house, so nobody outside that ring sees the ad. The creative shows the real room, the real coaches and the real members, because the alternative is stock footage of a gym that isn't his, and prospects in a small town can tell.

    How we run gym Facebook ads

  2. A page whose only job is a booked intro

    The traffic lands on a fast, mobile-first page with one action on it. No class timetable to get lost in, no eleven-item nav, no PDF download. Name, number, pick a time. Most gym websites are brochures. This one is a door.

    Gym website design

  3. Follow-up that does not wait for anyone to be free

    The moment someone enquires, automated SMS, email and voicemail go out, within minutes, not hours, and keep going for weeks if the person goes quiet. This is the unglamorous half of the job and it is where most gyms lose the sale. A lead that gets a reply in two minutes and a lead that gets one tomorrow morning are not the same lead.

    Gym lead follow-up

Josh's ad spend went from his own account, straight to Meta. We never touch it and we never mark it up. The build is deliberately short: campaigns and follow-up are usually live within days of kickoff, not weeks, because a gym waiting six weeks for a "strategy phase" is a gym paying for nothing.

Lessons for CrossFit gyms What this means for your box.

If you run an affiliate, the pattern is probably familiar: a real community, a coached room, growth that arrives in steps and then stalls. The fix at CrossFit Prior Lake wasn't a clever trick. It was local ads aimed at a radius people will actually drive, a page with one job, and follow-up that answers in minutes, run together by one person who answers for the members.
