Hire a gym marketing agency, or do it yourself?

We are an agency, so take this with the appropriate pinch of salt. But plenty of the gym owners who book a call with us should not hire anybody yet, and saying so on the call is cheaper for everyone than saying it in month four.

The question before the question

“Agency or DIY” assumes marketing is your constraint. Often it isn’t, and pouring traffic onto a gym with a different problem just makes the problem arrive faster and cost more. Three tests. Do them this week, before you price a single agency.

Test one: the retention test

Background reading: gym member retention past month six.

Open your billing system. Count the members who joined between nine and twelve months ago. Now count how many of those are still paying today. If that fraction is poor, more leads will not fix your revenue — you will spend money to refill a bucket with a hole in it, and in month six you will conclude that marketing does not work.

There is no universal pass mark here and anyone who gives you one is guessing at your town, your price point and your format. But you know what good looks like in your own business. If the honest answer is “most of them are gone”, fix onboarding and the first ninety days before you spend a dollar on ads.

Test two: the follow-up test

Have a friend fill in the enquiry form on your website on a Saturday afternoon. Time how long it takes for a human being to call them. Then count how many times you follow up before giving up.

If the answer is “Monday morning” and “twice”, you do not have a lead problem. You have a follow-up problem, and it is the cheapest problem on this list to fix. A printed script and a five-message text cadence will do more for your revenue this quarter than any campaign. We wrote the front desk sales script and the follow-up text templates out in full elsewhere on this blog precisely because most gyms can do them without hiring anybody.

Test three: the capacity test

If twenty-five new members walked in next month, where would they go? Is there a coach for the 5:30pm class? Is there floor space? If the honest answer is that your peak classes are already uncomfortable, marketing is not your next hire — a coach or a second time slot is.

The short version

Bad retention, no follow-up, or no capacity: do not hire an agency this quarter. None of those are marketing problems, and marketing spend on top of them converts your cash into other people’s ad impressions.

What doing it yourself actually costs

DIY is not free. It is paid for in the least replaceable thing you have, which is your attention on the gym floor. Be honest about the shape of it.

Setup, roughly one to two weeks of evenings. Business manager, ad account, pixel or conversions API, a page for the traffic to land on, a CRM that can at minimum text a lead automatically, and an offer you have actually written down. None of this is intellectually hard. All of it is fiddly, and platform interfaces change often enough that the tutorial you find will be half wrong.

Ongoing, three to five hours a week, every week. Filming and editing creative is the bulk of it and it never stops — ad fatigue is real and the ad that worked in March stops working. Then checking delivery, reading comments, and rewriting what is not landing.

Plus the part nobody counts: the phone. Whether you hire or not, the calls still get made by you or your staff. Agencies that promise to remove this from your life are selling you something that does not exist. The lead wants to talk to the gym, not to a marketing company in another state.

The real question is not “can I learn this” — you can, it is a skilled trade and not a secret one. It is “is the five hours a week I spend on ads worth more than the five hours I would otherwise spend coaching, selling and keeping members”. For a lot of owner-operators with a young gym, the answer is genuinely yes. DIY is the right call more often than agencies admit.

What hiring actually buys

You are buying three things and you should be clear which of them you need.

  • Time. The five hours a week comes back. This is the honest core of the value proposition for most gyms.
  • Pattern recognition. Someone who has watched a lot of gym campaigns knows within a week which of your three problems it is, where a solo operator burns a month finding out.
  • Infrastructure you keep. The follow-up automations, the landing page, the tracking. If your agency does not let you keep these, that is a deliberate choice on their part and you should read it as one.

You are not buying demand that does not exist in your town, and you are not buying a salesperson. Both of those get sold constantly. Neither is deliverable.

And there is a real cost beyond the retainer: ad spend goes on top, paid by you directly to the platform, and you should assume the first sixty to ninety days are partly diagnostic. Anything that works on day three was luck, and luck reverts. If you cannot afford three months of spend plus retainer without it hurting, you cannot afford to hire yet — not because agencies are slow, but because a two-month test does not produce a reliable answer and you will cancel just as the data becomes useful.

Five questions, answered honestly

Write your answers down. The pattern in them is the recommendation.

1. Can somebody at your gym sell?

When a stranger sits across the desk and asks the price, does someone in your building know how to handle that conversation without apologising for the number? If not, an agency makes things worse faster: more appointments, same close rate, more money spent per signed member, and a growing suspicion that the leads are junk.

2. Can you lose ninety days of ad budget without it hurting?

Not “will you” — can you. If the honest answer is no, the risk profile is wrong for a retainer. Start with the free channels and the member list you already have.

3. Do you have three to five hours a week, reliably?

Not this week, when you are motivated. In eight weeks, in the middle of a staffing problem. DIY marketing dies in week seven, not week one, and a campaign left unattended for a month spends money the whole time.

4. Have you actually exhausted the free stuff?

Most gyms have not. A claimed and maintained Google Business Profile with recent photos and a steady trickle of reviews. A structured referral ask at the ninety-day mark. A phone call — not an email — to every member who cancelled in the last eighteen months. Those three cost nothing and, in our experience, routinely outperform a first ad campaign.

If you have not done them, doing them first also gives you a baseline to judge the ads against.

5. Is your gym big enough for the maths to work?

Below roughly forty members, a monthly retainer is usually a large fraction of revenue and the gym does not yet have the coaching capacity, the review count or the social proof for ads to land well. It is not a moral failing, it is a stage. The same is true in very small towns, where you can genuinely exhaust the addressable audience inside a quarter.

The three honest answers

Answer A: don’t hire anyone yet

You picked this one if you failed the retention test, the follow-up test or the capacity test, or if question two was a no. Your next thirty days (and the longer version is in how to get more gym members):

  • Write the enquiry-call script and the in-person intro script. Print them. Role-play them with whoever works the desk until they stop sounding read.
  • Build a five-message text cadence so no enquiry waits more than five minutes.
  • Claim and fill out the Google Business Profile. Ask ten happy members for a review, by name, in person.
  • Call every lapsed member from the last eighteen months. Ask what happened. Do not pitch on the first call.
  • Fix the first ninety days of membership: an intake conversation, a check-in at day thirty, a check-in at day sixty.

Do that and either your revenue moves without ads, or you now know precisely what is broken. Both outcomes are worth more than a retainer.

Answer B: do it yourself

You picked this if you have the hours, you or a staff member can sell, the gym is small enough that a retainer is a stretch, and you actually enjoy this part. A workable DIY stack is smaller than people think: one Meta campaign with one offer, a simple page with a form, a CRM that texts instantly, a calendar link, and a habit of filming two pieces of creative a week on your phone. Keep it that small for ninety days, and judge it against your own gym Facebook ads cost per lead, not a number from somebody else’s town. Most DIY failure is not incompetence, it is trying to run six things at once.

Answer C: hire

You picked this if the gym is established, retention is decent, someone can sell, you have the budget to run a proper test, and the five hours a week is worth more to you on the floor. That is a real and common situation, and it is when an agency earns its fee rather than just occupying a line on your P&L. It is also what the numbers on our results page are: marketing added to a gym that already worked, not marketing used to rescue one. Josh at CrossFit Prior Lake went from 81 to 132 members.

If you hire: what to ask before you sign

Nine questions. The answers are more revealing than any case study deck.

  1. Whose ad account is it? It should be yours, with them as a user. If it is theirs, you leave with nothing — no history, no learnings, no audiences.
  2. Who owns the leads and the CRM? Same answer required.
  3. Do you mark up ad spend? It should go from your card to the platform, unmarked.
  4. How long is the contract? A long lock-in is a statement about how confident they are in month three.
  5. What will you report, and will it include members, not just leads? Ask specifically whether they report retention at month three and six.
  6. Will you work with the gym two miles from me? If the answer is anything other than no, understand that you are bidding against your own agency.
  7. Who, by name, will I actually speak to? Not a team, a person.
  8. What happens in week one? A specific answer beats an enthusiastic one.
  9. What would make you tell me this isn’t a fit? If they cannot name a scenario, they have never turned anyone down.

The hybrid most gyms land on

In practice the split that works is rarely all-or-nothing. The gym owns the things only the gym can do: the phone, the intro conversation, the content that makes the place look like itself, the ninety-day onboarding. The agency owns the things that reward repetition across many accounts: the ad account, the tracking, the follow-up automation, the landing page, and the monthly honest read of what the numbers mean.

If you hire and hand over the whole thing including the phone, it will not work, and it will not be obvious whose fault it is until a lot of money has gone.

If you have run the three tests and you are not sure which answer you landed on, that is a thirty-minute conversation, not a sales call. Bring your member count, your ninety-day retention and what you are spending now.
