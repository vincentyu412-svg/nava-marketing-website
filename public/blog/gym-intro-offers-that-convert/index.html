Gym intro offers that convert

Free trial, paid intro, challenge or consultation? Each one attracts a different person and converts in a different way. Here is how to choose, how to structure it, and how to follow it up so trials become members.

The short answer

The gym intro offers that convert best are the ones with a built-in conversation. A free trial brings the most people and converts the fewest unless you book a sit-down inside it. A paid intro brings fewer, more committed people. A challenge creates urgency but needs a plan for the day it ends. A free consultation plus workout gives the cleanest route to a membership. Whichever you pick, book a specific start time, keep it short, and decide the next step before they start.

Your intro offer is the most important sentence in your marketing. It goes in every ad, on the top of your website and in the first text a lead receives. It decides who replies, how many reply and how hard it will be to turn them into members. Yet most gyms pick one by copying the gym down the road, then blame the ads when it doesn’t convert.

This guide compares the four formats honestly, then gets practical: how to structure the offer, how to talk about price, and the exact follow-up that turns a trial into a membership.

What is a gym intro offer actually for?

An intro offer has one job: to turn a stranger into a person standing in your gym talking to a coach. It is not there to sell the membership. That happens in the conversation afterwards. When owners forget this, they design offers to be as generous as possible (“a free month!”), and end up with lots of people who train alone for four weeks, never speak to anyone and quietly stop coming.

So judge any offer on three questions. Does it make the first step feel low-risk for a nervous beginner? Does it get them in front of a coach quickly? And does it create a natural moment, before the offer runs out, to sit down and talk about a membership? An offer that does all three will outsell a more generous one that does none of them.

The four gym intro offers, compared

Almost every gym offer is a version of one of four formats. Each one attracts a different kind of person and asks something different of your staff. The ratings below are our judgement from running offers for gyms, not survey data, so treat them as a starting point to test against your own numbers.

The free trial (a free class, a free week)

The free trial is the default for a reason: it removes every barrier and brings the most enquiries. It also attracts the most people who were never going to buy. People who collect free weeks at every gym in town, people who are curious but not ready, people who wanted a free workout. Left unstructured, a free week means someone attends two classes, talks to nobody and disappears.

A free trial converts when you treat it as a wrapper around a consultation. Keep it short, a single session or a few days rather than a month. Make booking the first session with a named coach a condition of the trial, and book the sit-down conversation about membership before the trial ends, on the day they start. It suits gyms with lots of capacity and a low monthly price, where volume matters, such as 24-hour and larger open gyms.

The paid intro (a first session or intro pack for a small fee)

Charging something, even a small amount, changes who shows up. A person who has paid has already made a small decision to take this seriously, which is why paid intros tend to bring fewer leads but better ones who turn up more reliably. It works especially well for coaching-led gyms, personal training studios and CrossFit boxes, where the membership is a bigger commitment and the first sessions need a coach anyway.

Two things make a paid intro sell. First, the value is obvious: “three one-to-one sessions with a coach and a plan built for you” is easy to say yes to in a way “intro pack” isn’t. Second, the fee comes off the first month if they join, so the decision to become a member feels like continuing, not starting over.

The challenge (a four to eight week program)

A challenge sells an outcome and a start date: “a six-week kickstart that starts on the 3rd”. That combination creates real urgency and a group that starts together, which is good for community and for your coaches. Challenges can fill a gym quickly, particularly group fitness and HIIT studios with classes to fill.

The risk is the cliff at the end. People join a challenge, not a gym, and on the last day they have finished what they signed up for. Challenges convert when the transition to membership is part of the challenge from day one: a goal review booked for the final week, a members-only “what’s next” offer, and coaches who talk about “after the challenge” from week two. Without that plan, a challenge is an expensive way to run a six-week pop-up.

The free consultation plus workout

The consultation offer is the most honest format: “Come in, sit down with a coach for twenty minutes to talk about your goals, then try a session.” It brings fewer enquiries than a free week, but everyone who books has agreed to a conversation, which is the step that sells a membership. It costs you the least staff time per lead and gives the cleanest route to a yes, because the recommendation happens in the same visit.

It works for almost any coaching-led gym. Its weakness is that it can sound like a sales meeting, so the ad and the confirmation text need to make it sound like what it is: a coach helping them work out where to start.

Which intro offer should you choose?

Start with your membership, not your ads. The bigger and more personal the commitment you’re asking for, the more your offer should lean towards a conversation.

  • High-touch, higher-price coaching (personal training, small-group training, CrossFit): a free consultation plus workout, or a paid intro pack.
  • Class-based studios with capacity (HIIT studios, group fitness studios, bootcamp): a challenge with a start date, or a short paid intro, with the membership conversation built in.
  • Open gyms with a lower monthly fee (24-hour and access-controlled gyms): a short free pass with an induction or a quick orientation booked on day one.

Then pick one and stick with it long enough to measure. Running three offers at once splits your budget, confuses your staff and makes it impossible to tell which one worked. If your gym type isn’t listed, our fitness studio marketing page covers how we approach each format.

How to structure an intro offer

Whatever the format, the offers that convert share the same six rules:

  1. One offer, everywhere. The same words in the ad, on the landing page, in the confirmation text and at the front desk. Any mismatch feels like a bait and switch.
  2. A specific outcome, not a product. “Your first week, with a coach who’ll build your plan” beats “7-day pass”.
  3. A time box. Short enough that the decision comes while they are still excited. A long trial postpones the decision until the motivation has faded.
  4. A booked start, with a named coach. Not “come whenever”. A day, a time and a person.
  5. A conversation inside it. The sit-down about goals and membership is part of the offer, booked in advance, not an awkward ambush at the end.
  6. A limit that is true. Limited spots or a start date create urgency. Only use them if they are real, because people notice when the “last 5 spots” never run out.

How to talk about price without scaring people off

The fear of the price conversation ruins more intros than the price itself. A few principles make it easier for everyone:

  • Discount the first step, never the membership. A free or cheap intro lowers risk. A discounted membership lowers what every member is worth for as long as they stay, and trains your town to wait for the next promotion.
  • Price after the goal, not before. The price lands differently once they have told you what they want and seen how you would get them there. On an enquiry call, it is fine to give a range and explain it depends on what they need.
  • Offer two or three options, not a menu. A recommended option in the middle, one with more support and one with less. Recommend the one that fits their goal, out loud.
  • Credit the intro. If they paid for an intro, it comes off the first month. If it was free, the “start today” reason can be a waived joining fee or a bonus session, rather than a cheaper rate.
  • Say the number plainly, then stop. No apologising, no filling the silence. Let them respond.

The follow-up that turns trials into members

An intro offer without a follow-up plan is a free class. The follow-up starts the minute someone claims it and ends when they have either joined or said a clear no. Here is the timeline, with a template for each step. Change the names, keep the shape.

Within minutesText · book the start

Hi [first name], it’s [coach] from [gym]. Thanks for grabbing the [offer]! When suits you for your first session, [day] at [time] or [day] at [time]? It’s one-to-one with me for the first part, so no need to be fit already.

When they bookText · confirm everything

You’re booked for [day] at [time]. We’re at [address], parking is [where]. Wear something comfy and bring water. Ask for me, [coach], when you arrive. We’ll start with a quick chat about your goals, then a session. Reply here if you need to move it.

Same day, after the first sessionText · a win and the next visit

Great work today, [first name]. You [specific thing they did well]. Your next session is [day] at [time]. I’ve pencilled in 15 minutes on [day] to go over your plan and what would suit you after the [offer].

Mid-trialText · check in

How are the legs after [workout]? Honest answer, how’s it feeling so far? Anything you want more of, or less of, before we sit down on [day]?

After the plan talk, if undecidedText · no pressure, one clear step

Thanks for chatting today. No pressure at all. If it helps, the [recommended option] we talked about starts whenever you’re ready, and your [intro fee / joining fee] comes off. Want me to hold your spot in the [class/time] you liked?

Three rules make this work. Every message comes from a named person, from a number that can receive replies. Automation handles the reminders but stops the moment they reply, so a human carries the conversation. And the membership talk is booked, not improvised. If they don’t show for a session, rebook the same day with two new times, because most no-shows are embarrassment, not a decision.

Intro offer templates you can adapt

Fill in the brackets. Each one states who it is for, what they get, the limit and the next step.

  • Free consultation: “New to [gym type] or getting back into it? Book a free 20-minute goal session with a coach, then try a workout built for your level. No experience needed. Pick a time below.”
  • Paid intro: “Three one-to-one sessions with a coach for [price]. We’ll learn how you move, build your plan and get you ready for classes. If you join, the [price] comes off your first month.”
  • Challenge: “The [6]-week [name] starts [date]. Coached classes, a simple nutrition guide and a check-in with your coach every week. [Number] spots, because that’s how many we can coach properly.”
  • Free trial: “Try [gym] free for [3 days]. Your first visit is a 15-minute orientation with a coach so you know exactly what to do. Book your first visit to activate your pass.”

Mistakes that kill intro offer conversion

  • Trials that are too long. A month is long enough to lose motivation and short enough to feel like a favour. Shorter trials put the decision at the peak of their enthusiasm.
  • No named coach. People stay for people. If nobody learned their name in the first visit, the trial is just a free sample.
  • Selling at the door, not at the table. Asking for the sale on the way out, sweaty and rushed, feels pushy. A booked sit-down feels like help.
  • Changing the offer every month. You never collect enough data to know what works, and your staff never learn to sell any of them well.
  • Blaming the ads. If people claim the offer but don’t join, the leak is in the follow-up or the intro, not the campaign. Our guide on how to get more gym members shows how to find which stage is leaking.

Frequently asked questions

Is a free trial or a paid trial better for a gym?

A free trial brings more enquiries. A paid trial brings fewer people who are more committed and show up more reliably. For coaching-led gyms with a higher-priced membership, a paid intro or a free consultation usually converts better. For open gyms with a lower monthly fee, a short free pass with a booked orientation often works well.

How long should a gym free trial be?

Shorter than most gyms think: a single session, a few days or a week. A short trial puts the membership decision while the person is still motivated. Long trials, like a free month, let the enthusiasm fade before anyone asks them to decide.

How do you convert a gym trial into a membership?

Book the conversation in advance. Start the trial with a named coach, text after each session, check in halfway, and schedule a short sit-down to talk about their goals and the right membership before the trial ends. Recommend one option, state the price plainly and ask.

Do gym challenges work?

They can fill a gym quickly because they combine an outcome with a start date. They only turn into members if the move to membership is planned from day one, with a goal review booked in the final week and a clear offer for what comes next.
