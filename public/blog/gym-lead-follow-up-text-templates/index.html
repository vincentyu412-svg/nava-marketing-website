Gym lead follow-up text templates: the whole cadence.

Six rules that make the difference

The templates below are ordinary. That is deliberate — the reason most gym follow-up fails is not the wording, it is the timing, the volume and the fact that it reads like a company. Six rules first, because a good message sent at the wrong time is worse than an average one sent at the right one.

1. The first message goes out in minutes, not hours

Someone who fills in a form at 9:40pm is at the peak of their intent at 9:41pm. By Monday morning they have looked at two other gyms and remembered they are busy. The first text should be automated for exactly this reason: no human is reliably available at 9:41pm, and a human is not needed for the first message.

2. One ask per message

Every text below asks for exactly one thing, and the thing is nearly always a yes/no or an A/B. “Let me know if you have any questions” asks for nothing and gets nothing.

3. Short, lower case where natural, no marketing voice

No exclamation marks stacked up, no all-caps, no emoji as punctuation, no “Great news!”. It should read like it came from a person standing in a gym, because it did. Two lines is usually enough.

4. Signed by a person, from a number that receives replies

“— Sam at [Gym]” on the first message and then never again. If your texts go out from a number nobody monitors, the whole exercise is theatre. Someone should be checking it during opening hours.

5. Stop the automation the second they reply

The fastest way to burn a lead is to send them the scheduled 72-hour “still thinking about it?” text after they already booked. Whatever tool you use must halt the sequence on any inbound message. Test this yourself before you turn it on.

6. Get the consent and the opt-out right

In the United States, marketing texts to a mobile number are governed by the TCPA and FCC rules, which require consent before autodialed or prerecorded calls and texts and give the recipient the right to revoke that consent in any reasonable way — see the FCC’s own consumer guide to unwanted calls and texts. In Canada, CASL governs commercial electronic messages including SMS, and requires consent, sender identification and a working unsubscribe in every message; the CRTC publishes the act, regulations and guidelines.

Practically, for a gym: put a clear consent line next to your form (“we’ll text you about your enquiry”), include “Reply STOP to opt out” on the first message of any sequence and on the dormant re-engagement, honour STOP instantly and permanently, and keep a record of when and how each person opted in. If you are sending from a business number in the US, your provider will also need you registered for application-to-person messaging before anything delivers reliably. None of this is legal advice — check your own setup with someone qualified.

Fill these in once

Every template below uses square brackets for the bits you swap: [First Name], [Gym], [Staff], [Offer], [Link], [Coach], [Class time]. Do a find-and-replace once, save them in your CRM, and never rewrite them from scratch again.

The ask

Starting now, or looking around?

One ask per message. The sequence stops the second they reply.

Stage 1 — five minutes: the one that matters most

Automated, instant, and phrased so a reply is easier than ignoring it.

Why this works: It asks a question with two easy answers, neither of which is a commitment. “Just looking” is a perfectly good reply and it tells you exactly which follow-up to run. A message ending in “let us know if you’d like to book!” gets silence.

Alongside it, call them. The text is not a substitute for the phone in the first ten minutes — it is what catches the ones who do not pick up. The enquiry call script is the other half of this, word for word.

Stage 2 — same day, no reply

Two options, never “when are you free”. And the second one lowers the bar: some people will not book but will ask a question, and a question is a live lead.

Stage 3 — 24 hours

By now they have not replied to three touches. Change the shape of the message: stop asking for the booking, give them something.

Name the objection before they do. In the threads we read, the real hesitation is more often intimidation than price, and a message that says so out loud gets replies from people who would never have raised it themselves.

Stage 4 — 72 hours

Naming two acceptable reasons makes it easy to answer honestly — you have handed them a socially acceptable excuse, so replying costs nothing. Expect a good share of the answers to be some version of “sorry, been mad at work”, which is a live lead you would otherwise have written off.

Stage 5 — day 7: close the loop

Asking permission to close the file is the easiest message in the sequence to answer, because “yes, close it” costs the recipient nothing and ends the thing they have been avoiding. That is why it tends to shake loose the people who went quiet. Send it — and when someone says close it, actually close it.

The ones that book: confirmation and reminders

Booking is not showing up. Three messages between the yes and the appointment.

The “if something’s come up just say” line feels like it invites cancellations. It does the opposite of what you fear: it converts silent no-shows into rescheduled appointments, and a rescheduled appointment is still a lead.

The no-show sequence

A no-show is not a rejection, it is a Tuesday that got away from someone. Treat it as an admin problem and most of them rebook. The tone must contain zero guilt. (If your no-shows cluster around one kind of offer, read which gym intro offers convert.)

Waking up a dead list

Every gym has a list of a few hundred people who enquired months ago and never joined. Most owners consider it dead. It is not dead, it is stale, and the reason they did not join in March has often stopped being true.

Two cautions before you send anything to this list. First, check they have not opted out. Second, if the consent is old or you cannot prove it exists, do not blast the list — re-permission it or call instead. The rules referenced above apply to old leads exactly as they apply to new ones.

Note what message one does not do: it does not offer a discount and it does not announce a new promotion. It asks whether to remove them. That question earns a reply from people who would ignore an offer, and the replies sort your list into live and dead in about forty-eight hours.

Send it in batches

Do not text four hundred people at nine on a Monday morning. You will get forty replies in an hour, answer eight of them badly, and lose the rest. Fifty a day, sent mid-morning, with someone assigned to answer. The bottleneck is your ability to reply, not your ability to send.

What to do when they reply

The templates get you the conversation. The conversation is where the sale is, and the rules are simple.

  • Reply within minutes during opening hours. A reply that arrives the next day restarts the whole problem.
  • Move to a call or a booking within two messages. Texting is a scheduling tool, not a sales channel. “Easier to explain in two minutes on the phone — alright if I ring you now?”
  • Answer the price question with a real number and then a question. “[$X] a month for [three] days a week, no contract. Want me to hold you a spot Tuesday so you can see if it’s worth it?”
  • Never negotiate by text. If they want a discount, that is a conversation with a human being, in the building.
  • Log the outcome. Booked, not now, or dead. A cadence with no outcome field turns into a list nobody trusts within a month.

Automate the sending, not the conversation

Automate: the five-minute text, the hour and five-hour nudges, the 24-hour and 72-hour messages, the day-seven close, all three appointment reminders, and the first no-show text. Those are timing problems and software is better at timing than you are.

Do not automate: the replies, the price conversation, the rebooking after a second no-show, or anything to a person who has already spoken to you. The moment a real human says something back, a real human answers. Gyms that automate the reply as well end up with a system that is efficient at annoying people.
